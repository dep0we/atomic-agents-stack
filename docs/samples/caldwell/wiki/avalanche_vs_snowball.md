## Avalanche method

**Mechanic:** Pay minimums on every debt. Pour every extra dollar into the highest-interest-rate balance until it's zero. Move to next-highest. Repeat until debt-free.

**Strength:** Mathematically optimal. Minimizes total interest paid over the lifetime of the debts.

**Weakness:** First "win" can take a long time if the highest-rate debt is also the largest balance. Easy to lose momentum without visible progress markers.

**Best for:** People comfortable with delayed gratification, motivated by math, with stable income that can sustain the long grind on the first balance.

## Snowball method

**Mechanic:** Pay minimums on every debt. Pour every extra dollar into the smallest balance until it's zero. Move to next-smallest. Repeat until debt-free.

**Strength:** Psychologically optimal. Produces visible "wins" early as small balances disappear. Builds motivation and confirms the system is working.

**Weakness:** Pays more total interest than avalanche, often by a meaningful amount over multi-year horizons.

**Best for:** People who need motivation reinforcement, have multiple small balances, or have ever fallen off a debt-payoff plan because progress felt invisible.

## When to use which

Conventional advice (e.g., Ramsey) defaults to snowball because it improves *adherence* — the best plan is the one you actually finish. Modern personal-finance research (Brown & Lahey 2015, Forbes Advisor analysis 2024) suggests this generalization holds: snowball-completing-population > avalanche-attempting-but-stalling-population.

For people with high adherence (consistent income, no impulse spending, can grind on a long-running first balance), avalanche wins on total interest paid.

## Hybrid approach

Some advisors recommend a hybrid: list debts by rate, but if there's a tiny balance you can clear in 1-2 months, do that first to establish the win, then switch to avalanche. Sometimes called "snow-valanche" or "hybrid avalanche."
